First, the equivalence is shown between the methods of Section~\ref{sec:mathematical_framework}, and second, the levers for harmonic recombination are investigated, both on the IEEE 30-bus system. The IEEE 30-bus system has a meshed 132\,kV transmission level and a 33\,kV distribution level. Bus~1 is the only reference bus, at~$1.0\angle0^{\circ}$\,pu at the fundamental; the other generator buses are voltage-controlled buses, and no bus is an ideal harmonic ground. At the harmonics, the generators are represented by their subtransient impedance, with a short-circuit power of~2\,GVA for the generators at buses~1 and~2 and~0.2\,GVA for the others, at a reactance-to-resistance ratio of~20, as in~\cite{vanacker_impact_2025}. The line charging of the original data is removed, so that the base network is purely inductive, as the overhead-line case of~\cite{vanacker_impact_2025}%
%: with it, the line charging forms a parallel resonance with the network inductance near~$\Harmonic = 9.4$, i.e., a capacitive element in disguise
. %
%Without line charging, the network cannot be operated within the original reactive power limits of its generators and synchronous condensers.
 The fundamental operating point therefore follows from an ac optimal power flow with bus~1 fixed at~1.0\,pu, the branch ratings and the original voltage limits of~0.94--1.05\,pu enforced, and the reactive power limits of all generators lifted. The harmonics~$\HarmonicSet = \{5,7,11,13\}$ %
%, characteristic of the 6-pulse converter of Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty},
 are limited by the individual and total harmonic distortion planning levels and the root-mean-square voltage and current limits. The harmonic units are the first~$N$ loads, with $N = 21$ unless stated otherwise, under the absolute equality fairness criterion, at a risk level~$\varepsilon = 0.05$. All results are in pu on a 100\,MVA basis%
%, and each subsection below has its own script in \textsc{HarmonicPowerModels.jl}
.

\subsection{Equivalence of the Methods}
\label{sec:equivalence}

Table~\ref{tab:equivalence} compares the methods of Section~\ref{sec:mathematical_framework} on the base network. When every angle range collapses to its mean, the uncertainty vanishes and the second-order cone program~\eqref{eq:socp} reproduces the deterministic model of~\cite{vanacker_hosting_2026}. With uncertainty, the second-order cone program and the general quadratically constrained program agree to within~$10^{-7}$ with the empirical risk factor%
%, i.e., the local solution of the non-convex program is the global one in these cases
; the former is solved with \textsc{Mosek}, the latter with the local solver \textsc{Ipopt}, which limits it to~$N = 4$. Finally, the hosting capacity reduced to the individual harmonic voltage limits~\eqref{eq:reduced_hosting} equals the deterministic model at the mean angles and, for the exact~95\,\% quantile, the second-order cone program with the empirical risk factor, which confirms that these limits bind and that the reduced metrics of Section~\ref{sec:levers} describe the full model.

\subsection{Levers for Harmonic Recombination}
\label{sec:levers}

With a single hosting capacity per harmonic and binding individual harmonic voltage limits, as in all cases below, the hosting capacity at harmonic~$\Harmonic$ is set by the tightest bus,
\begin{IEEEeqnarray}{ r C l }
    \HostingCapacity_{\Harmonic} &=& \min_{\Node \in \NodeSet} \frac{U^{\text{ihd}}_{\Node,\Harmonic}\,|\ComplexVoltage_{\Node,1}|}{A_{\Node,\Harmonic}}, \qquad c_{\Node\Unit,\Harmonic} = Z_{\Harmonic,\Node\Node(\Unit)}\,e^{\im\phi_{\Unit,\Harmonic}}, \label{eq:reduced_hosting}
\end{IEEEeqnarray}
where~$A_{\Node,\Harmonic}$ aggregates the unit contributions~$c_{\Node\Unit,\Harmonic}$ per unit of current by one of four rules: (i)~arithmetic summation~$\sum_{\Unit}|c_{\Node\Unit,\Harmonic}|$, i.e., no recombination; (ii)~the robust bound~$\max_{\bar{\omega}}|\sum_{\Unit}c_{\Node\Unit,\Harmonic}|$ over the support of the germ; (iii)~the mean angle, $|\sum_{\Unit}c_{\Node\Unit,\Harmonic}|$ at~$\Expectation[\bar\omega]$, as in~\cite{vanacker_hosting_2026}; and (iv)~the 95\,\% chance bound, i.e., the exact $(1-\varepsilon)$-quantile of~$|\sum_{\Unit}c_{\Node\Unit,\Harmonic}|$. The equivalent summation exponent~$\alpha^{\text{eq}}_{\Harmonic}$ is the exponent for which the summation law of IEC~61000-3-6~\cite{noauthor_iec61000-3-6_2008},
\begin{IEEEeqnarray}{ r C l }
    A_{\Node,\Harmonic} &=& \Big(\sum_{\Unit \in \HarmonicUnitSet} |c_{\Node\Unit,\Harmonic}|^{\alpha^{\text{eq}}_{\Harmonic}}\Big)^{1/\alpha^{\text{eq}}_{\Harmonic}}, \label{eq:summation_law}
\end{IEEEeqnarray}
reproduces the hosting capacity of rule~(iv); $\alpha^{\text{eq}}_{\Harmonic} = 1$ is arithmetic summation, and the indicative values of the standard are~1.4 for~$5 \leq \Harmonic \leq 10$ and~2 for~$\Harmonic > 10$.

Fig.~\ref{fig:populations} shows the result. The network sets the level of the hosting capacity: the 33\,kV cables lower the 95\,\% hosting capacity by~44--48\,\% for all three populations, through the resonances near the 7th and 13th harmonic, and raise the gain over arithmetic summation, e.g., from 4 to~12\,\% for the diode bridges. Near a resonance, the transfer impedances from the units to a bus rotate apart, which spreads their contributions. A single converter technology recombines little, Fig.~\ref{fig:populations}(a): the 95\,\% hosting capacity lies 4--12\,\% above arithmetic summation for the diode bridges and~12--24\,\% for the thyristor bridges, whose angles are spread more widely. The equivalent summation exponent stays between~1.01 and~1.22, Fig.~\ref{fig:populations}(b)--(c), and the summation law with the indicative exponents of IEC~61000-3-6 overestimates the hosting capacity by a factor of~2.0--2.8. A mix of both technologies is the condition under which substantial recombination occurs. The mean angles of the two bridges differ by~92--152$^{\circ}$ at the characteristic harmonics, so their contributions partly cancel, and the 95\,\% hosting capacity rises~62\,\% above arithmetic summation in the base network and~88\,\% with the cables. The exponent then reaches~1.39--1.46 at the 7th and 11th harmonic, where the mean angles nearly oppose each other, and~1.10--1.33 at the 5th and 13th harmonic; the summation law still overestimates the hosting capacity by a factor of~1.4--1.7, as the high-order exponent remains below the indicative~2.0. The offset follows from the time shift of the current waveform, Section~\ref{sec:converter_types}: the mean fundamental current of the thyristor bridges lags that of the diode bridges by about~20$^{\circ}$, which rotates the $\Harmonic$-th harmonic by about~$\Harmonic \cdot 20^{\circ}$, i.e., 99$^{\circ}$, 139$^{\circ}$, 218$^{\circ}$ and~257$^{\circ}$, so that the two populations nearly oppose each other at the 7th and 11th harmonic only. The harmonics at which the mix recombines most therefore depend on the firing delay of the thyristor bridges: a shift of~5$^{\circ}$ in their mean fundamental angle already moves the largest gain from the 11th to the 7th or the 13th harmonic. At the 13th harmonic, the wide angle range of the thyristor bridges also shortens their mean phasor to~0.56, so that their contributions cannot balance those of the diode bridges.

\subsubsection{Comparison with the Summation Law and the Literature}
\label{sec:comparison}

The results identify the conditions under which harmonic recombination raises the hosting capacity:
\begin{enumerate*}
    \item[(i)] differences in mean angle between units, from different converter technologies, which is the dominant mechanism: a mix of diode and thyristor bridges raises the 95\,\% hosting capacity by~62--88\,\% above arithmetic summation;
    \item[(ii)] the rotation~$\Harmonic\,\theta_{\Node}$ of the local angle reference, which grows with the harmonic order and with the spread of the fundamental voltage angles; and
    \item[(iii)] capacitive elements that rotate the transfer impedances apart around a resonance, which raises the gain over arithmetic summation, e.g., from 4 to~12\,\% for the diode bridges.
\end{enumerate*}
The dispersion of the angles around a common mean yields only little recombination by itself, 4--24\,\% over arithmetic summation here, as the angles of a single converter technology are concentrated, with standard deviations of~6--16$^{\circ}$ for the diode bridge and~21--60$^{\circ}$ for the thyristor bridge. The benefit of the chance constraint is its gain over the robust bound, 3--24\,\% for one technology and~58--83\,\% for the mix, where the mean angle is not a safe design value.

With one converter technology, the equivalent summation exponent is close to one at every harmonic, and the indicative exponents of IEC~61000-3-6 overestimate the hosting capacity by a factor of~2.0--2.8. This agrees with the measurements of Wang et al.\ in residential distribution systems, where low-order harmonic currents from different homes add up with little cancellation~\cite{wang_characteristics_2017}, and with the caution of Santos et al.\ that the indicative exponents may overestimate the aggregate of correlated sources~\cite{santos_considerations_2015}. Cho et al.\ found that deterministic emission limits can drive the voltage distortion to twice the planning levels~\cite{cho_analysis_2019}; the summation law, applied to a population of one converter technology, has the same effect in the opposite direction. With a mix of diode and thyristor bridges, the exponent reaches the indicative~1.4 at the 7th harmonic, but stays at~1.10--1.11 at the 5th harmonic, and the high-order exponent remains at~1.23--1.46, below the indicative~2.0. Wang et al.\ measured cancellation mainly at the higher orders and their residential loads combine many technologies, and the angle spread of each converter grows with the harmonic order, which the present 6-pulse models capture only in part. The high-order exponent is therefore the result most sensitive to the converter models of Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty}, including whether the firing delay of the thyristor bridges is common to all units.
