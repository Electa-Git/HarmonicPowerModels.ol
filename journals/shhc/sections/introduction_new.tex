\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:background_and_motivation}

\IEEEPARstart{R}{eaching} a net-zero economy by 2050 requires extensive electrification of the energy system. Over the past two decades, renewable generation, particularly wind and photovoltaic power, has grown steadily and is expected to accelerate towards 2050~\cite{international_energy_agency_world_2023}. At the same time, decarbonization targets necessitate the electrification of transport, heating, and industrial processes, replacing fossil fuels with electricity. In Europe, studies project that total electricity demand will triple by 2050~\cite{energyville_paths_2023}. Consequently, future power systems will be largely dominated by power-electronic interfaced resources on both the supply and demand sides. This transition fundamentally changes the harmonic behavior of power systems. \removable{Traditional grids, dominated by synchronous machines and mostly linear loads, exhibited limited harmonic distortion. Modern systems, however, increasingly rely on converters whose nonlinear characteristics generate a broad spectrum of harmonic currents.} The aggregation of numerous distributed converters creates new challenges in maintaining harmonic voltage quality across transmission and distribution networks.

A key challenge arises from the stochastic nature of harmonic emissions. Measurements and laboratory studies show that harmonic magnitudes depend on loading, switching states, and internal control actions. More importantly, harmonic current phase angles, which govern the phasor summation of emissions from multiple sources, vary significantly. Even small angle variations can result in large differences in harmonic voltage distortion, particularly in networks with many distributed converters. As converter penetration increases, system operators need transparent metrics to assess whether additional units can be connected without exceeding harmonic limits. Harmonic hosting capacity provides such a metric by defining the maximum allowable harmonic emissions while ensuring compliance with power-quality standards. However, most existing methods rely on deterministic assumptions regarding harmonic magnitudes, phase angles, network parameters, and background distortion, leading to either overly conservative or insufficiently protective results.

This work presents a harmonic hosting capacity methodology that explicitly accounts for the stochastic nature of harmonic current phase angles associated with power-electronic interfaced resources, thereby capturing harmonic recombination effects. This enables the enforcement of stochastic limits on harmonic voltages and currents in line with relevant standards.

\subsubsection{Standards and the summation law}

The standard IEC~61000-3-6 allocates planning levels over the installations connected to a point of common coupling and aggregates their emissions with a general summation law, $I_{\Harmonic} = (\sum_{\Unit} I_{\Unit,\Harmonic}^{\alpha})^{1/\alpha}$, with indicative exponents $\alpha = 1$ for $\Harmonic < 5$, $1.4$ for $5 \leq \Harmonic \leq 10$ and $2$ for $\Harmonic > 10$, and compares the 95\,\% values of the distortion with the planning levels~\cite{noauthor_iec61000-3-6_2008}. The summation law thus carries the diversity of the harmonic phase angles into the standard, but its exponents are indicative. Santos et al.\ used the summation law to determine the hosting capacity of transmission and distribution systems, represented the upstream network by a Norton equivalent with background distortion of unknown phase, and obtained a hosting capacity between 37\,MW and 160\,MW for the in-phase and anti-phase extremes; they also cautioned that the indicative exponents may under- or overestimate the aggregate of correlated sources such as wind and photovoltaic plants~\cite{santos_considerations_2015}. Field measurements confirm this caution: Wang et al.\ analyzed synchronized measurements in North-American residential distribution systems and found that low-order harmonic currents from different homes tend to add up, i.e., the cancellation is much less than claimed in the literature, while cancellation occurs at high orders only~\cite{wang_characteristics_2017}. Cho et al.\ compared IEC~61000-3-6 with the deterministic current limits of IEEE Standard~519, set as a function of the short-circuit ratio~\cite{noauthor_ieee519_2022}, by Monte Carlo simulation on a multi-feeder distribution model, and showed that the latter can drive the voltage distortion of distribution systems up to twice the planning levels~\cite{cho_analysis_2019}. \removable{Trelles-Molina et al.\ extended the summation law from transmission systems to individual customer facilities with few harmonic sources and analyzed the probabilities that support the exponents~\cite{trellesmolina_extension_2022}.} How much recombination a given network and converter population actually allow therefore remains an open question.

\subsubsection{Stochastic harmonic power flow}

Probabilistic harmonic power flow quantifies the uncertainty of the harmonic emissions and of the network response. Li et al.\ separated aleatory and epistemic uncertainty sources, defined quantitative amplification indices as ratios of transfer to driving-point impedances, and assessed them with Monte Carlo simulation~\cite{li2020}. \removable{Monte Carlo and data-driven methods have since become the standard tool for probabilistic harmonic power flow: Xie and Sun proposed a piecewise probabilistic harmonic power flow for unbalanced residential distribution systems~\cite{xie_piecewise_2022}, Xie et al.\ a data-driven approach for distribution systems with photovoltaic generation~\cite{xie2022}, and Cadena-Zarate et al.\ a simulation-based approach for low-voltage networks with distributed energy resources~\cite{cadenazarate2024}.} These methods quantify uncertainty, but do not optimize the hosting capacity.

\subsubsection{Stochastic harmonic hosting capacity}

Early harmonic hosting capacity methods incorporated harmonic limits within deterministic frameworks. Sun et al.\ incorporated harmonic voltage constraints into an optimal power flow for distribution network capacity assessment~\cite{sun_distribution_2012} and extended it to active networks~\cite{sun_incorporating_2012}, and Sakar et al.\ analyzed the hosting capacity of large photovoltaic plants in the presence of background distortion~\cite{sakar_integration_2018}. Sun and Harrison proposed the first optimization-based stochastic harmonic hosting capacity method, with percentile-based harmonic distortion limits as chance constraints within an ac optimal power flow, and showed that relaxing the risk level increases the admissible hosting capacity~\cite{sun_distribution_2022}. Nakhodchi et al.\ included the measured emission uncertainty of a fast charging station for electric vehicles through Bayesian statistics and the correlation between harmonic orders~\cite{nakhodchi2022}, and showed that the estimated hosting capacity depends strongly on how the medium-voltage network and the remote loads are modeled~\cite{nakhodchi_impact_2023}. The present authors determined an exact lower bound for the equitable harmonic hosting capacity with a second-order cone formulation of the full network, but with fixed harmonic current angles~\cite{vanacker_hosting_2026}, and studied the impact of underground cables on it~\cite{vanacker_impact_2025}.

Table~\ref{tab:literature_review} shows that no existing method simultaneously achieves: (i)~tractable network-wide optimization, (ii)~explicit treatment of phase angle uncertainty, and (iii)~chance-constraint enforcement of harmonic distortion limits. Moreover, the recombination that a network and a converter population allow has not been quantified against the summation law of IEC~61000-3-6, and the conditions under which it occurs have not been identified.

\subsection{Contributions and Outline}
\label{sec:contribution_and_outline}

In this paper, a holistic one-shot methodology is presented to determine the steady-state harmonic hosting capacity for an entire phase-balanced three-phase power system in a stochastic context. The presented method considers uncertainty on the angle of the injected harmonic current, enabling harmonic recombination, and limits the resulting individual and total harmonic voltage distortion, as well as the root-mean-square voltages and currents, using chance constraints. The problem is formulated from the perspective of the power system operator, i.e., without explicit knowledge of the future assets of its customers. Consequently, the harmonic hosting capacity is determined in terms of harmonic current magnitude using a fairness principle. The presented formulation uses the rectangular current-voltage variable space and incorporates:
\begin{itemize}
    \item uncertainty on the angle of the injected harmonic current via polynomial chaos expansion, with one random variable per unit and one shared by all units of a converter type, angles referenced to each unit's fundamental terminal voltage, and an exact projection of the trigonometric functions,
    \item exact representation of the network impedance in the frequency domain based on the harmonic impedance of individual grid components,
    \item detailed treatment of transformers including the effects of their vector group configuration,
    \item enforcement of stochastic harmonic limits given in relevant standards on bus voltage and component ampacity through chance constraints with a Cantelli, Gaussian or empirically calibrated risk factor, and
    \item equitable distribution of the harmonic hosting capacity following a fairness principle.
\end{itemize}
For fairness criteria that fix the ratios between the allocations of the units, e.g., absolute equality, the problem reduces to a second-order cone program with one pair of variables per harmonic. Furthermore, metrics are proposed to quantify harmonic recombination, i.e., the gain over a robust bound and an equivalent summation exponent that is directly comparable with IEC~61000-3-6, and the conditions for recombination are identified. The models and numerical illustrations are made freely available as part of the \textsc{Julia}-package \textsc{HarmonicPowerModels.jl}.\footnote{Available at \url{https://github.com/timmyfaraday/HarmonicPowerModels.jl}.}
