\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:background_and_motivation}

\IEEEPARstart{R}{eaching} a net-zero economy by 2050 requires extensive electrification of the energy system. Over the past two decades, renewable generation—particularly wind and photovoltaic (PV) power—has grown steadily and is expected to accelerate towards 2050~\cite{international_energy_agency_world_2023}. At the same time, decarbonization targets necessitate the electrification of transport, heating, and industrial processes, replacing fossil fuels with electricity. In Europe, studies project that total electricity demand will triple by 2050~\cite{energyville_paths_2023}. Consequently, future power systems will be largely dominated by power-electronic interfaced resources on both the supply and demand sides. This transition fundamentally changes the harmonic behavior of power systems. \removable{Traditional grids, dominated by synchronous machines and mostly linear loads, exhibited limited harmonic distortion. Modern systems, however, increasingly rely on converters whose nonlinear characteristics generate a broad spectrum of harmonic currents.} The aggregation of numerous distributed converters creates new challenges in maintaining harmonic voltage quality across transmission and distribution networks.

A key challenge arises from the stochastic nature of harmonic emissions. Measurements and laboratory studies show that harmonic magnitudes depend on loading, switching states, and internal control actions. More importantly, harmonic current phase angles—which govern the phasor summation of emissions from multiple sources—vary significantly. Even small angle variations can result in large differences in harmonic voltage distortion, particularly in networks with many distributed converters. As converter penetration increases, system operators need transparent metrics to assess whether additional units can be connected without exceeding harmonic limits. Harmonic hosting capacity provides such a metric by defining the maximum allowable harmonic emissions while ensuring compliance with power-quality standards. However, most existing methods rely on deterministic assumptions regarding harmonic magnitudes, phase angles, network parameters, and background distortion, leading to either overly conservative or insufficiently protective results.

This work presents a harmonic hosting capacity methodology that explicitly accounts for the stochastic nature of harmonic current phase angles associated with power-electronic interfaced resources, thereby capturing harmonic recombination effects. This enables the enforcement of stochastic limits on harmonic voltages and currents in line with relevant standards.

\subsubsection{Standards and the summation law}

The standards determine harmonic emission limits at a single point of common coupling. IEEE Std.~519 prescribes deterministic current limits as a function of the short-circuit ratio~\cite{noauthor_ieee519_2022}, whereas IEC~61000-3-6 allocates planning levels over the installations and aggregates their emissions with a general summation law, $I_{\Harmonic} = (\sum_{\Unit} I_{\Unit,\Harmonic}^{\alpha})^{1/\alpha}$, with indicative exponents $\alpha = 1$ for $\Harmonic < 5$, $1.4$ for $5 \leq \Harmonic \leq 10$ and $2$ for $\Harmonic > 10$~\cite{noauthor_iec61000-3-6_2008}. Cho et al.\ compared both standards for distribution systems and showed that the deterministic emission limits of IEEE Std.~519 can drive the voltage distortion up to twice the planning levels, which the stochastic aggregation of IEC~61000-3-6 avoids~\cite{cho_analysis_2019}. The summation law thus carries the diversity of the harmonic phase angles into the standards, but its exponents are indicative. \removable{Trelles-Molina et al.\ extended the law from transmission systems to individual customer facilities with few harmonic sources and analysed the probabilities that support the exponents~\cite{trellesmolina_extension_2022}.} Santos et al.\ used the summation law to determine the hosting capacity of transmission and distribution systems, represented the upstream network by a Norton equivalent with background distortion of unknown phase, and obtained a hosting capacity between 37\,MW and 160\,MW for the in-phase and anti-phase extremes; they also cautioned that the indicative exponents may under- or overestimate the aggregate of correlated sources such as wind and photovoltaic plants~\cite{santos_considerations_2015}. Field measurements confirm this caution: Wang et al.\ analysed synchronized measurements in North-American residential distribution systems and found that low-order harmonic currents from different homes tend to add up, i.e., the cancellation is much less than claimed in the literature, while cancellation occurs at high orders only~\cite{wang_characteristics_2017}. How much recombination a given network and converter population actually allow therefore remains an open question.

\subsubsection{Stochastic harmonic power flow}

Probabilistic treatment of harmonic currents dates to the early 1990s. Pileggi et al.\ characterized harmonic current emissions from nonlinear loads as random variables and studied their statistical aggregation in distribution feeders~\cite{pileggi1993}. Bollen and Gu demonstrated that random phase angles significantly reduce expected harmonic voltages relative to deterministic in-phase assumptions, underscoring the role of phase angle diversity~\cite{bollen2006}. \removable{Monte Carlo methods later became the standard tool for probabilistic harmonic load flow. Rodríguez-Pajarón et al.\ proposed a piecewise probabilistic harmonic power flow for unbalanced residential distribution systems~\cite{rodriguez2022}, Xie et al.\ a data-driven approach for distribution systems with photovoltaic generation~\cite{xie2022}, and Cadena-Zarate et al.\ a simulation-based approach for low-voltage networks with multiple types of distributed energy resources~\cite{cadenazarate2024}.} Uncertainty also resides in the network response: Li et al.\ separated aleatory and epistemic uncertainty sources, defined quantitative amplification indices as ratios of transfer to driving-point impedances, and assessed them with Monte Carlo simulation~\cite{li2020}. These methods quantify uncertainty, but do not optimize the hosting capacity.

\subsubsection{Stochastic harmonic hosting capacity}

Early harmonic hosting capacity methods incorporated harmonic injections within deterministic frameworks. Sakar et al.\ analysed the hosting capacity of large photovoltaic plants considering background distortion~\cite{sakar_integration_2018}, and Sun et al.\ formulated non-linear harmonic hosting capacity models for active distribution networks~\cite{sun_distribution_2012, sun_incorporating_2012}. Nakhodchi et al.\ included the uncertainties of a fast charging station for electric vehicles through Bayesian statistics and the correlation between harmonic orders, and found that the harmonic phase angles are essentially uncorrelated with the magnitudes~\cite{nakhodchi2022}; they also showed that the estimated hosting capacity depends strongly on how the medium-voltage network and the remote loads are modelled~\cite{nakhodchi_impact_2023}. Sun and Harrison proposed the first optimization-based stochastic harmonic hosting capacity method, with probabilistic harmonic distortion limits as chance constraints within an AC optimal power flow, and showed that relaxing the risk level increases the admissible hosting capacity~\cite{sun_distribution_2022}. The present authors determined an exact lower bound for the equitable harmonic hosting capacity with a second-order cone formulation of the full network, but with fixed harmonic current angles~\cite{vanacker_hosting_2026}, and studied the impact of underground cables on it~\cite{vanacker_impact_2025}.

\subsubsection{Research gaps}

Table~\ref{tab:literature_review} shows that no existing method simultaneously achieves: (i)~tractable network-wide optimization, (ii)~explicit treatment of phase angle uncertainty, and (iii)~chance-constraint enforcement of harmonic distortion limits. Moreover, the recombination that a network and a converter population allow has not been quantified against the summation law of IEC~61000-3-6, and the conditions under which it occurs have not been identified.

\subsection{Contributions and Outline}
\label{sec:contribution_and_outline}

In this paper, a holistic one-shot methodology is presented to determine the steady-state harmonic hosting capacity for an entire phase-balanced three-phase power system in a stochastic context. The presented method considers uncertainty on the angle of the injected harmonic current, enabling harmonic recombination, and limits the resulting individual and total harmonic voltage distortion, as well as the root-mean-square voltages and currents, using chance constraints. The problem is formulated from the perspective of the power system operator, i.e., without explicit knowledge of the future assets of its customers. Consequently, the harmonic hosting capacity is determined in terms of harmonic current magnitude using a fairness principle. The presented formulation uses the rectangular current-voltage variable space and incorporates:
\begin{itemize}
    \item uncertainty on the angle of the injected harmonic current via polynomial chaos expansion, with one germ per unit, one germ shared by all units of a converter type, angles referenced to each unit's fundamental terminal voltage, and an exact projection of the trigonometric functions,
    \item exact representation of the network impedance in the frequency domain based on the harmonic impedance of individual grid components,
    \item detailed treatment of transformers including the effects of their vector group configuration,
    \item enforcement of stochastic harmonic limits given in relevant standards on bus voltage and component ampacity through chance constraints with a Cantelli, Gaussian or empirically calibrated risk factor, and
    \item equitable distribution of the harmonic hosting capacity following a fairness principle.
\end{itemize}
For the absolute equality fairness criterion, the problem reduces to a second-order cone program with one pair of variables per harmonic. Furthermore, metrics are proposed to quantify harmonic recombination, i.e., the gain over a robust bound and an equivalent summation exponent that is directly comparable with IEC~61000-3-6, and the conditions for recombination are identified. The models and numerical illustrations are made freely available as part of the \textsc{Julia}-package \textsc{HarmonicPowerModels.jl}.\footnote{Available at: \href{https://github.com/timmyfaraday/HarmonicPowerModels.jl}{https://github.com/timmyfaraday/HarmonicPowerModels.jl}}
