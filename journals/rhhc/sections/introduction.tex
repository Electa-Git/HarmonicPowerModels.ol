\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:background_and_motivation}

\IEEEPARstart{R}{eaching} a net-zero economy by 2050 requires extensive electrification of the energy system. During the past two decades, renewable generation, particularly wind and photovoltaic power, has grown steadily and is expected to accelerate towards 2050~\cite{international_energy_agency_world_2023}. At the same time, decarbonization targets require the electrification of transport, heating, and industrial processes, replacing fossil fuels with electricity. In Europe, studies project that the total electricity demand will triple by 2050~\cite{energyville_paths_2023}. Consequently, future power systems will be largely dominated by power-electronic interfaced resources on both the supply and demand sides. This transition fundamentally changes the harmonic behavior of power systems. Traditional grids, dominated by synchronous machines and mostly linear loads, exhibited limited harmonic distortion. Modern systems, however, increasingly rely on converters whose nonlinear characteristics generate a broad spectrum of harmonic currents. The aggregation of numerous distributed converters creates new challenges in maintaining harmonic voltage quality across transmission and distribution networks.

A key challenge arises from the stochastic nature of harmonic emissions. Measurements and laboratory studies show that harmonic magnitudes depend on loading, switching states, and internal control actions. More importantly, harmonic current phase angles, which govern the phasor summation of emissions from multiple sources, vary significantly. Even small angle variations can result in large differences in harmonic voltage distortion, particularly in networks with many distributed converters. As the penetration of the converter increases, system operators need transparent metrics to assess whether additional units can be connected without exceeding harmonic limits. Harmonic hosting capacity provides such a metric by defining the maximum allowable harmonic emissions while ensuring compliance with power-quality standards. However, most existing methods rely on deterministic assumptions regarding harmonic magnitudes, phase angles, network parameters, and background distortion, leading to overly conservative or insufficiently protective results.

\subsubsection{Standards and the summation law}

The standard IEC~61000-3-6 allocates planning levels over installations connected to a point of common coupling and aggregates their emissions with a general summation law, $I_{\Harmonic} = (\sum_{\Unit} I_{\Unit,\Harmonic}^{\alpha})^{1/\alpha}$, with indicative exponents $\alpha = 1$ for $\Harmonic < 5$, $1.4$ for $5 \leq \Harmonic \leq 10$ and $2$ for $\Harmonic > 10$, and compares the 95\,\% distortion values with the planning levels~\cite{noauthor_iec61000-3-6_2008}. The summation law thus carries the diversity of the harmonic phase angles into the standard, but its exponents are indicative. Santos et al.\ used the summation law to determine the harmonic hosting capacity of transmission and distribution systems, represented the upstream network by a Norton equivalent with background distortion of unknown phase, and obtained a harmonic hosting capacity between 37\,MW and 160\,MW for the in-phase and anti-phase extremes; they also cautioned that the indicative exponents may under- or overestimate the aggregate of correlated sources such as wind and photovoltaic plants~\cite{santos_considerations_2015}. Field measurements confirm this caution: Wang et al.\ analyzed synchronized measurements in North-American residential distribution systems and found that low-order harmonic currents from different homes tend to add up, i.e., the effects is much less than claimed in the literature, while recombination occurs at high orders only~\cite{wang_characteristics_2017}. Cho et al.\ compared IEC~61000-3-6 with the deterministic current limits of IEEE Standard~519, set as a function of the short-circuit ratio~\cite{noauthor_ieee519_2022}, by Monte Carlo simulation on a multi-feeder distribution model, and showed that the latter can drive the voltage distortion of distribution systems up to twice the planning levels~\cite{cho_analysis_2019}. Trelles-Molina et al.\ extended the summation law from transmission systems to individual customer facilities with few harmonic sources and analyzed the probabilities that support the exponents~\cite{trellesmolina_extension_2022}. How much recombination a given network and converter population actually allow therefore remains an open question.

\subsubsection{Stochastic harmonic power flow}

Probabilistic harmonic power flow quantifies the uncertainty of the harmonic emissions and the associated network response. Li et al.\ separated aleatory and epistemic uncertainty sources, defined quantitative amplification indices as ratios of transfer to driving-point impedances, and assessed them with Monte Carlo simulation~\cite{li2020}. Monte Carlo and data-driven methods have since become the standard tool for probabilistic harmonic power flow: Xie and Sun proposed a piecewise probabilistic harmonic power flow for unbalanced residential distribution systems~\cite{xie_piecewise_2022}, Xie et al.\ a data-driven approach for distribution systems with photovoltaic generation~\cite{xie2022}, and Cadena-Zarate et al.\ a simulation-based approach for low-voltage networks with distributed energy resources~\cite{cadenazarate2024}. These methods quantify uncertainty, but do not optimize the hosting capacity.

\subsubsection{Stochastic harmonic hosting capacity}

Early harmonic hosting capacity methods incorporated harmonic limits within deterministic frameworks. Sun et al.\ incorporated harmonic voltage constraints into an optimal power flow for distribution network capacity assessment~\cite{sun_distribution_2012} and extended it to active networks~\cite{sun_incorporating_2012}, and Sakar et al.\ analyzed the hosting capacity of large photovoltaic plants in the presence of background distortion~\cite{sakar_integration_2018}. Sun and Harrison proposed the first optimization-based stochastic harmonic hosting capacity method, with percentile-based harmonic distortion limits as chance constraints within an ac optimal power flow, and showed that relaxing the risk level increases the admissible hosting capacity~\cite{sun_distribution_2022}. Nakhodchi et al.\ included the measured emission uncertainty of a fast charging station for electric vehicles through Bayesian statistics and the correlation between harmonic orders~\cite{nakhodchi2022}, and showed that the estimated hosting capacity depends strongly on how the medium-voltage network and the remote loads are modeled~\cite{nakhodchi_impact_2023}. The present authors determined an exact lower bound for the equitable harmonic hosting capacity with a second-order cone formulation of the full network, but with fixed harmonic current angles~\cite{vanacker_hosting_2026}, studied the impact of underground cables on it~\cite{vanacker_impact_2025}, and extended it to stochastic harmonic current angles with chance constraints, which reduces to a second-order cone program with one pair of variables per harmonic and quantifies the recombination of the units~\cite{vanacker_shhc}.

\subsubsection{Network parameter uncertainty}

All of the above take the network parameters as known, whereas the harmonic impedance that sets the hosting capacity is uncertain. The joint CIGRE and CIRED guide on network harmonic impedance stresses that it changes continuously with load and network configuration, recommends representative operating conditions or worst-case impedance curves, and limits the validity of network models to about the 20th harmonic~\cite{robert_guide_1997}. Li et al.\ classified the uncertainty of the harmonic response into aleatory fluctuations, among which the drift of the component parameters with temperature, and epistemic component parameter uncertainty from manufacturing tolerances and installation, and propagated both by Monte Carlo simulation~\cite{li2020}. Bollen and R\"onnberg presented the hosting capacity as a tool for planning under uncertainty and noted that acceptable harmonic models exist for cables, lines and transformers, but that several barriers remain before a complete model is available for hosting capacity studies~\cite{bollen_hosting_2017}. The sensitivity of the hosting capacity to the network representation is large: the modeling of the medium-voltage network and the remote loads~\cite{nakhodchi_impact_2023} and of underground cables~\cite{vanacker_impact_2025} changes it substantially, as both move the parallel resonances, whose location and sensitivity to the network parameters follow from resonance mode analysis~\cite{xu_harmonic_2005,huang_application_2007}. At transmission level, a Monte Carlo study under varying operating conditions found the harmonic hosting capacity to be governed by resonance-sensitive impedance~\cite{emini_study_2026}. These works sample or scan the network parameters, but none of them optimizes the hosting capacity with a guarantee over their uncertainty, and none derives that uncertainty from the physical construction of the cables and lines.

\subsubsection{Robust optimization}

Robust optimization requires a constraint to hold for every realization of uncertain data within a set, and replaces the uncertain program by its robust counterpart, which is tractable for linear programs with ellipsoidal sets~\cite{ben-tal_robust_1999}. Where no tractable counterpart exists, cutting-set methods alternate between a master problem over finite scenario sets and a worst-case analysis~\cite{mutapcic_cutting-set_2009}. In power systems, robust optimization has mainly addressed the uncertainty of renewable generation and load~\cite{roald_power_2023}, e.g., through the affinely adjustable robust counterpart of the multi-period optimal power flow~\cite{jabr_robust_2015}. Lubin et al.\ combined both paradigms in a robust chance-constrained optimal power flow, which enforces chance constraints for every probability distribution whose parameters lie in an uncertainty set, solved by a cutting-plane algorithm~\cite{lubin_robust_2016}, and Margellos et al.\ placed robust optimization with randomized uncertainty bounds between the robust and the scenario approach~\cite{margellos_road_2014}. In these works, the uncertain data enter the constraints affinely or through the moments of a distribution, whereas the network parameters enter the harmonic hosting capacity through the inverse of the harmonic admittance matrix, which is far from concave near a parallel resonance.

\subsubsection{Research gaps}

Table~\ref{tab:literature_review} shows that tractable network-wide optimization with an explicit treatment of the uncertainty of the harmonic current angles and chance-constraint enforcement of the harmonic distortion limits is available~\cite{vanacker_shhc}, but that no existing method simultaneously achieves:
\begin{enumerate*}
    \item [(i)] such an optimization of the harmonic hosting capacity with a guarantee over the uncertainty of the network parameters, i.e., robustly rather than by sampling,
    \item [(ii)] network parameters derived from the physical construction of the cables and lines and their frequency dependence, rather than from scaled fundamental-frequency data, and
    \item [(iii)] the joint treatment of both uncertainties, the angles in probability and the network in the worst case.
\end{enumerate*}
Moreover, the zero-sequence harmonics, for which the transformer windings and earthing set the network, are absent from the optimization-based methods.

\subsection{Contributions and Outline}
\label{sec:contribution_and_outline}

In this paper, a holistic one-shot methodology is presented to determine the steady-state harmonic hosting capacity for an entire phase-balanced three-phase power system in a stochastic context. The presented method considers uncertainty on the angle of the injected harmonic current, enabling harmonic recombination, and limits the resulting individual and total harmonic voltage distortion, as well as the root-mean-square voltages and currents, using chance constraints. The problem is formulated from the perspective of the power system operator, who may anticipate the converter technologies its customers will connect, but not the operating point, parameters and control of the individual units. The angle of the harmonic current is therefore modeled as a random variable per unit, characterized for the anticipated technology, whereas its magnitude is the decision, and the harmonic hosting capacity is determined in terms of harmonic current magnitude using a fairness principle. The presented formulation uses the rectangular current-voltage variable space and incorporates:
\begin{itemize}
    \item uncertainty on the angle of the injected harmonic current via polynomial chaos expansion,
    \item exact representation of the harmonic impedance in the frequency domain and detailed treatment of transformers,
    \item enforcement of stochastic harmonic limits on bus voltage and component ampacity through chance constraints, and
    \item equitable distribution of the harmonic hosting capacity following a fairness principle.
\end{itemize}
For fairness criteria that fix the ratios between the allocations of the units, e.g., absolute equality, the problem reduces to a second-order cone program with one pair of variables per harmonic. %
The models and numerical illustrations are made freely available as part of the \textsc{Julia}-package \textsc{HarmonicPowerModels.jl}.\footnote{Available at \url{https://github.com/timmyfaraday/HarmonicPowerModels.jl}.}
